Working title
A LAN party game for 2–4 players: short physics mini-games, one shared scoreboard.
Concept render, AI-generated. Not gameplay.
Ice Rumble is a working title. It is a party game for two to four players on one local network: short physics mini-games, with points carried on one scoreboard. The four of us are the four player slots at the bottom. All game images in this deck are AI concept renders made from our own reference art; none of them is gameplay.
How it plays: one room, short rounds, one scoreboard
2–4 friends in one room, each at their own PC. One PC hosts; the others join over the local network. Everyone plays the same cube, in one of four colours.

Plain-language summary first: two to four friends, each on a PC, in the same room; one PC hosts. Everyone plays the same cube in one of four colours. The session loop. The host owns the lobby and the round order. Only points carry over between rounds; mass and position reset, so a bad round never makes the next one harder. We also plan catch-up scoring: whoever won the last round earns slightly fewer points in the next one, and whoever came last earns slightly more, so trailing players stay in contention until the final. Placement points 4, 3, 2, 1 and the size of the adjustment are proposals we will tune in playtests.
One value is your health, your ammunition and your weight
Shooting, getting hit and getting launched all read from one number the player can see: the cube's size.
Bars show direction only. Costs and knockback values are set in playtests.
One resource does three jobs. Shooting spends it: the cube spits out a piece of its own body, hit or miss. Getting hit loses it and pushes you back. And the less you have, the further the same hit sends you; off the edge is a knockout. We present the rule, not the numbers: the exact costs are tuning values we will set in playtests.
Why both the shooter and the target lose mass
A shot only pays off when it lands, so accuracy becomes the skill. And never shooting scores nothing: points come from knockouts, not survival.
Exact costs are tuning values. We fix them in playtests, not on paper.
Why the rule charges both sides. If only the shooter paid, shooting would be pure loss and hiding would win. If only the target paid, shooting would be free and spamming would win. Charging both makes each shot a trade: a hit should take more from the target than the shot cost you, and a miss costs only you. So aim and timing become the skill. The obvious objection, why not just wait and stay heavy, is answered by scoring: points come from knockouts, not survival, so a cube that never shoots scores nothing. Respawn at full mass and a round timer handle the rest.
Mini-game 01
Mass
Knockout
FIG 2 · Orange spits a piece of itself; Cyan is hit near the edge. Concept render, AI-generated. Not gameplay.
Mass Knockout. One flat platform with open edges. Spitting costs you mass; hits cost the target mass and push it; lighter cubes fly further. The last cube to hit someone gets the knockout. Rounds are timed and ranked by knockouts. Respawns are at full mass.
Mini-game 02
Ice
Race
FIG 3 · Racing a floating ice track; the HUD is part of the render. Concept render, AI-generated. Not gameplay.
Ice Race. No shooting here; the skill is momentum. Input sets acceleration, not velocity, and sideways grip is low, so you have to set up corners early. Braking restores grip. Falling sends you back to the last checkpoint: you lose time, not the race. Finish order gives placement, and anyone unfinished at the timeout is ranked by track progress.
One shared core; each mini-game plugs into it
Why this split
Reuse
A new mini-game is new rules plus one arena. Character, camera, network and scores already exist.
Per-game physics
Each mini-game sets its own profile: low grip on ice, firm grip in Knockout.
One truth
Everything that decides a result runs on the host, so every screen agrees.
Architecture. The mini-games sit on top of a shared core. Each one implements the same small contract: set up with the players, run, report placement. Below that sit the character, camera, round state machine, scoreboard and lobby, then the LAN layer, where the host is authoritative. Unity Netcode for GameObjects is our candidate library, not a final choice.
One shot over the LAN, step by step
Your own movement is predicted locally, so it feels instant. Hits, mass changes and knockouts are decided only on the host. Main risk: keeping physics smooth on clients. LAN latency is low, and we tune the snapshot rate.
One shot end to end. The client only sends intent. The host checks the cooldown, takes mass from the shooter, simulates the glob, applies the hit and knockback, and sends one snapshot to everyone. Clients interpolate. Own movement is predicted locally for feel; anything that decides a result lives on the host.
Social PC play is growing; LAN party games are rare
Steam “Party Game” titles by multiplayer tag
Steam store search, Oct 2026 [4]. Tags are user-applied.
Risk, stated plainly
An estimated 65.9% of 2025 Steam releases earned under $1,000 [5], while 5,863 games passed $100K in 2025 (Valve) [6]. LAN is our differentiator, not a demand driver.
Market potential. PC is a 43.6 billion dollar market that grew 12 percent in 2025; games with co-op made an estimated 4.1 billion on Steam in the first half of 2025; most players play with others. Only about 8 percent of Steam party games list LAN. Revenue is very uneven: most releases earn little, but thousands pass 100 thousand dollars. LAN is how we stand out, not why people buy.

Our core rule is that losing mass makes the next incoming hit launch you further. Spend your body to shoot, lose body when hit, that body is your size, ammunition and health, and less of it means you fly further. Same rules, different builds: weight is also ammunition
FIG 4 · Heavy, balanced and light builds of the same character. Concept render, AI-generated.
Weight classes are standard in the genre (e.g. Mario Kart). The twist: here weight is also your ammunition, so a build changes how you play, not only how you look.
Framework for after the MVP. Pips are relative; stats are not designed yet.
A framework, not a finished design. Characters can differ in starting mass and speed. Weight classes are common in the genre; what is different here is that mass is also ammunition, so a heavy build has more shots and a light build fewer. We would tune this after the two MVP mini-games work.
Prototype: the mass rule simulated before any Unity code
Drafts and prototypes. This is a real prototype: a deterministic physics simulation of the Mass Knockout rule, written in Three.js and rendered frame by frame. Over three shots the target shrinks and is pushed further each time. We built it to check that the escalating knockback reads clearly on screen before writing any Unity code.

Build order: shared core first, then each mini-game
Build order. The shared core comes first, then the LAN layer, then the two mini-games, Ice Race first because it reuses more of the core, Mass Knockout as the riskier one. Then the session loop and tuning. The order is fixed; milestone dates and owners are still to be decided. Below, the three main risks: networked physics, handled by host authority; balance, handled by scoring knockouts, full-mass respawns, a timer and catch-up scoring; and scope, handled by keeping the MVP to exactly two mini-games.
