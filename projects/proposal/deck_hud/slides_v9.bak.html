Shooter Party
Working title
October 2026
A LAN party game for 2–4 players. Shoot, push and blast your friends through short mini-games; one shared scoreboard decides the winner.
Shooter Party is a working title. It is a LAN party game for two to four players: shoot, push and blast your friends through short mini-games, and one shared scoreboard decides the winner. The four of us are at the bottom, with the roles we propose. The illustrations in this deck were made for our pitch; the 3D images are AI concept renders. None of them show gameplay.
Game description
Every round is a new mini-game, and placement turns into points.
Shooter Party is a party game for 2–4 friends in one room, each at their own PC. One player hosts and the others join over the local network. Everyone controls a small shooting cube. The mini-games change what a shot does: one pushes, one damages, one lifts you with a blast. After the last mini-game, the player with the most points wins.
Session settings
Placement points · proposed
Only points carry over between mini-games.
Plain-language summary. Two to four friends, each on a PC, in one room; one hosts. Every round is a new mini-game, and the shot does something different in each. A session runs lobby, pick, play, place, standings, and repeats. The host sets the rules: the session ends after a set number of mini-games or when someone reaches a target score; the playlist is random or custom, mini-games can be left out or repeated; and a mini-game can run rounds of its own, like Hole Grab losing one hole each round. Placement points 4, 3, 2, 1 are a proposal.
Core mechanics
One shooting cube. Every mini-game changes what a shot does.
The player. A small character with a barrel that fires real projectiles: physics objects, not instant hits. It does not have to be a cube, but its proportions should stay close to 1:1:1. Four colours: orange, cyan, lime, purple.
Concept render, AI-generated. Not gameplay.
One trigger, three effects
One shooting cube everywhere: a small character with a barrel that fires real physics projectiles. What a shot does depends on the mini-game. Damage lowers the target's health. Knockback pushes the target away from the direction of the shot. Blast explodes, and fired at the ground it lifts the shooter. WASD to move; jumping is on in some mini-games and off in others. The camera is a third-person shooter camera by default, and a mini-game can change it, like the 2.5D side view in Blast Parkour. Two mechanic ideas we want to try: shoot the ground to jump, and steer with recoil on ice.
Knockback Arena
Shoot the others off a flat floor. Every hit makes them lighter.
MVP
Illustration, not gameplay. Arena theme and size are not set.
Mass, not size: the same shot, a longer flight
Knockback Arena. A flat floor with open edges. Shooting is free with a fire-rate limit; shots are real thrown objects and do nothing to the shooter. A hit pushes the target from the shot's direction and lowers its mass, while its size stays the same. So the same shot pushes a weakened player further: at full mass a hit pushes a little; after a few hits it is a longer push; after many, one more shot sends it off the edge. Falling off means you are out. Our proposal: placement by order of elimination, last cube standing wins.
Blast Parkour
A 2.5D obstacle course: spit, blast and climb while knocking the others into the void.
Stretch goal
Illustration, not gameplay. Orange blasts the ground to jump; purple charges a strong shot; cyan has been knocked into the void.
If hitting is too easy
We make shots fall with gravity, so players have to read the arc to land a hit. We decide this in playtests.
Blast Parkour, a 2.5D obstacle course. It is built side-on, so there is nowhere to hide and everyone is always in someone's line of fire. Holding the button fires a strong explosion, but while charging you cannot move, so you are open to attack. A shot at the ground lifts you up, and the same blast can push a nearby rival off a ledge. Whoever drops below the course is out; the placement rule comes after the first tests. If hitting turns out too easy, shots fall with gravity. This one is our stretch goal for the final phase.
Ice Loop
Most checkpoints before the timer runs out wins. The ice does not help.
MVP
Concept render, AI-generated. Not gameplay. Ice is the theme of this one mini-game only.
Ice Loop. The goal is to pass the most checkpoints before the timer runs out. When the track ends it starts again, and every checkpoint you pass counts plus one; the count, not the lap, decides the placement. The ice is slippery: push rivals off it into the water and they restart from their last checkpoint. One idea we want to try: firing forward while sliding pushes you back, so a shot can also correct your line. Ice is the theme of this one mini-game, not of the whole game.
Hole Grab
Musical chairs with holes. No hole, and the bulldozer gets you.
MVP
Illustration, not gameplay. Each hole has the player's shape and a fixed barrel angle. Orange is already in a hole; the bulldozer moves in from the right.
Rounds inside the mini-game (4 players)
Hole Grab: musical chairs with holes. Holes open in the ground in the player's shape, with the barrel's angle marked, so you have to line up to drop in. Every round there is one hole fewer than players: four players and three holes, then three and two, then two and one. Whoever is left without a hole is crushed by the bulldozer. Shots push other players away from the holes, but a player already inside a hole cannot be pushed out.

Technical design
One shared core; every mini-game plugs into it.
Architecture. The four mini-games, and the next one, sit on a shared core. Each implements the same small contract: set up with the players and the session settings, run, report placement. Below that is shared gameplay: the cube controller with WASD and jump, the third-person camera that a mini-game can override, the shot system whose effect each mini-game sets, the session and playlist with end rules and exclusions, the scoreboard, and the lobby and UI. Then the LAN layer, where one player hosts and the others are clients; Unity Netcode is our candidate, not a final choice. The engine is Unity with built-in rigidbody physics.
One shot in Knockback Arena, over the LAN
The host decides; every screen agrees.
Your own movement is predicted locally, so it feels instant. Everything that decides a result runs on the host, so every screen agrees. Main risk: keeping physics smooth on clients; LAN latency is low, and we tune the snapshot rate.
One shot end to end. Client A sends fire input and aim direction. The host checks the fire-rate limit and spawns the projectile; the shooter is not affected. The physics step carries it to B. The host applies this mini-game's effect: B loses mass and is pushed from the shot's direction. The host sends the same snapshot, with positions, mass and scores, to every client, and clients interpolate between snapshots. Own movement is predicted locally for feel.
Market potential & target audience
Social PC play is growing; LAN party games are rare.
Our audience is groups of 2–4 friends who play together in one room, each on their own PC: students, LAN parties and game nights. They want short, readable rounds, a session they can shape themselves, and a game that works without an internet connection.

Risk, stated plainly
An estimated 65.9% of 2025 Steam releases earned under $1,000 [5], while 5,863 games passed $100K in 2025 (Valve) [6]. LAN is our differentiator, not a demand driver.
Market and audience. We aim at groups of two to four friends in one room, each on their own PC: students, LAN parties and game nights. PC is a 43.6 billion dollar market that grew 12 percent in 2025; games with co-op made an estimated 4.1 billion on Steam in the first half of 2025; most players play with others. Only about 8 percent of Steam party games list LAN. Revenue is very uneven, so LAN is how we stand out, not why people buy.

Positioning. Each competitor covers part of this table. Squarena weakens your body by shrinking it; we lower mass instead, and none ties knockback to lost mass. None advertises official LAN. The Shooter Party column is our plan, not shipped software.
Innovation & creativity
No shipped game we found ties knockback to the mass you have lost.
Most party games pick one: a playlist of unrelated mini-games, or one physics brawler. Shooter Party keeps one character and one shot across the whole session, and lets each mini-game bend what that shot means.
Three things are new together. First, mass, not size: hits lower your mass while your size stays, so the same shot launches a weakened player further and everyone can see the danger build. Second, one trigger with many effects: the same shot pushes, damages or lifts you depending on the mini-game, so controls are learned once and each mini-game still feels new. Third, your session, your rules: random or custom playlists, excluded or repeated mini-games, and an end by rounds or by score, all on a LAN with no internet.
Closest prior art
What exists already, and what we did not find.
Prior-art search of shipped games, Oct 2026 [9–10]. “Not found” means not found after searching.
We name the relatives. Squarena weakens your body in 2D, and Morphies Law moves mass between robots. Smash Bros. raises knockback with a damage percentage counter, not with mass. Blasting yourself upward is classic rocket jumping, as in Quake and Team Fortress 2. Recoil movement exists in some games; we have not searched shoot-to-steer on ice yet. Lighter-means-launched-further, and mass knockback in a LAN party game, we did not find.
Physics prototype
Does knockback read on screen?
A real prototype: a deterministic physics test in Three.js. Over three shots the target flies further each time, and the rule is readable without any UI. What changed since: this early test showed lost mass as shrinking. Our rule now keeps the size and lowers only the mass, so the Unity test needs another way to show it, a HUD bar, a colour or an effect, still to be chosen.

The plan. The proposed MVP is the shared core plus three mini-games: Knockback Arena, Ice Loop and Hole Grab. Blast Parkour joins in the final phase if time allows. Four Monday checkpoints: the proposal on 19 October; Demo 1 on 16 November with the shared core, LAN host and join and a playable Knockback Arena; Demo 2 on 14 December with Ice Loop, Hole Grab and a full session; the final on 11 January with tuning, polish and the promo video.
