Ice Rumble is a working title. It is a party game for two to four players on one local network: short physics mini-games, with points carried on one scoreboard. All game images in this deck are AI concept renders made from our own reference art; none of them is gameplay.
01 — The game
ICE RUMBLE / PROPOSAL
A LAN party game made of short physics mini-games
FIG. 1 — End of a round: placement decides points. Concept render, AI-generated.
Players
2–4 people in one room, each on their own PC. One PC hosts; the others join over the local network. No internet, no accounts.
Session
A playlist of short mini-games. Placement in each round earns points; one scoreboard runs across the whole session.
Characters
Everyone plays the same cube character, in one of four colours, with the same controls in every mini-game.
MVP scope
Two mini-games, Mass Knockout and Ice Race, on one shared Unity core.
02
Plain-language summary. Who: two to four friends, each on a PC, in the same room, one PC hosts. What: a session is a run of short mini-games; your place in each round gives points; the scoreboard decides the night. Scope: two mini-games for the course, both built on the same shared core.
02 — Session flow
ICE RUMBLE / PROPOSAL
How a session runs: lobby, rounds, standings
Lobby
Host starts a session. Others join with the host’s local IP.
Round
Host picks the next mini-game. Everyone loads the same arena.
Play
Host runs the physics. Clients send input and render the result.
Place
Placement becomes points: 4 / 3 / 2 / 1 (proposed).
Standings
Scoreboard updates. After the last round: overall winner.
Next mini-game
Between rounds
Points
The only thing that accumulates across the session.
Catch-up scoring
Last round’s winner earns slightly fewer points next round; last place earns slightly more. Nobody drops out of the race before the final.
Nothing physical
Mass and position reset every round, so one bad round does not snowball.
03
The session loop. The host owns the lobby and the round order. Only points carry over between rounds; mass and position reset, so a bad round never makes the next one harder. We also plan catch-up scoring: whoever won the last round earns slightly fewer points in the next one, and whoever came last earns slightly more, so trailing players stay in contention until the final. Placement points 4, 3, 2, 1 and the size of the adjustment are proposals we will tune in playtests.
03 — Core mechanic
ICE RUMBLE / PROPOSAL
Mass is your health, your ammo and your weight
1
2
3
Shoot
A cube spits out a piece of its own body. Every shot makes it smaller, hit or miss.
Hit
The target loses mass on impact and is pushed back along the line of the shot.
Fall
Less mass means the same hit pushes further. Off the edge is a knockout.
FIG. 2 — Three moments of one exchange in Mass Knockout. Concept render, AI-generated. Not gameplay.
04
One resource does three jobs. Shooting spends it, getting hit loses it, and the less you have, the further you fly. We present the rule, not the numbers: the exact costs are tuning values we will set in playtests.
04 — Design reasoning
ICE RUMBLE / PROPOSAL
Why both the shooter and the target lose mass
If only the shooter paid
Shooting is pure loss. The best play is to never shoot.
Players wait for others to weaken each other.
✕ Rewards hiding
If only the target paid
Shooting is free. The best play is to shoot nonstop.
Aim stops mattering; volume wins.
✕ Rewards spam
Both pay — our rule
Every shot is a trade. A miss costs only you.
A hit should take more from the target than the shot cost the shooter.
✓ Rewards aim and timing
Spending mass only pays off when the shot lands, so accuracy becomes the skill.
Exact costs are tuning values. We fix them in playtests, not on paper.
05
Why the rule charges both sides. If only the shooter paid, hiding would win. If only the target paid, spamming would win. Charging both makes each shot a trade that only pays off when it lands, so aim and timing become the skill.
04 — Design reasoning
ICE RUMBLE / PROPOSAL
Can a player win by never shooting?
The risk
A cube that never shoots keeps its mass, so it stays heavy and hard to push. If the last cube standing won, waiting could be the best strategy.
Our answer: score knockouts, not survival
Points come from knockouts. A cube that never shoots scores nothing, however heavy it is.
Respawn at full mass. A knocked-out cube returns after a short delay, so falling behind does not snowball.
Short round timer. Waiting out the clock earns no points.
Fallback if playtests still show stalling: a shrinking arena, or letting cubes re-absorb spent globs.
Same hit, less mass, more knockback
Heavy: hard to push — but a cube that waits scores nothing.
Light: the same hit launches it off the edge.

MINIMUM

06
The obvious objection: shooting costs mass, so why not just wait? A waiting cube does stay heavy and hard to push. But points come from knockouts, not survival, so waiting scores nothing. Respawning at full mass stops snowballing, and a short timer removes the stall. If playtests still show stalling, we have two fallbacks: shrink the arena, or let cubes re-absorb spent globs.
05 — Mini-game 1
Mass
Knockout
Goal
Knock other cubes off the floating platform.
Controls
Move, aim, spit. A short cooldown between shots.
Scoring
The knockout goes to the last cube that hit the one that fell. Most knockouts at the timer wins.
Respawn
After a short delay, at full mass.
Arena
One flat square platform. Open edges, no walls.
FIG. 3 — Orange spits a piece of itself; Cyan is hit near the edge. Concept render, AI-generated.
Mass Knockout. One flat platform with open edges. Spitting costs you mass; hits cost the target mass and push it; lighter cubes fly further. The last cube to hit someone gets the knockout. Rounds are timed and ranked by knockouts. Respawns are at full mass.
06 — Mini-game 2
Ice
Race
Goal
Finish the laps first on a floating ice track.
Controls
Steering changes acceleration, not velocity. Braking restores grip.
Skill
Low sideways grip: carry speed through corners without sliding off.
Falls
Respawn at the last checkpoint. You lose time, not the race.
Placement
Finish order; unfinished players ranked by progress.
FIG. 4 — Racing a floating ice track. Concept render, AI-generated.
Ice Race. No shooting here; the skill is momentum. Input sets acceleration, sideways grip is low, so you have to set up corners early. Falling sends you back to the last checkpoint. Finish order gives placement, and anyone unfinished at the timeout is ranked by progress.

09
Architecture. The mini-games sit on top of a shared core. Each one implements the same small contract: set up with the players, run, report placement. Below that sit the character, camera, round state machine, scoreboard and lobby, then the LAN layer, where the host is authoritative. Unity Netcode for GameObjects is our candidate library, not a final choice.

12
Our core rule is that losing mass makes the next incoming hit launch you further. We searched for this and did not find it in any shipped game. The other links have relatives, and we name them: Osmos and Agar.io spend body mass, but to move; Squarena shrinks you as you lose health, in 2D; Morphies Law moves mass between robots; Crisol spends your blood as ammunition. Smash Bros. raises knockback with a percentage counter, not with mass. Mass Knockout puts all four links on one physical value that the player can see.

Stalling or snowballing. Score knockouts; respawn at full mass; catch-up scoring.

Build order. The shared core comes first, then the LAN layer, then the two mini-games, with Mass Knockout as the riskier one, then the session loop and tuning. Milestone dates and owners are still to be decided. Three main risks, each with its mitigation.
